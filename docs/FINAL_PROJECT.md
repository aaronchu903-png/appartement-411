# L’Appartement 411 - Final Project

## 1. The destination

**A living pixel world in which people learn French by having a life.**

The player moves into a Paris apartment, gets to know two roommates, studies, works, tends a plant and gradually becomes part of a neighborhood. Language is how the player understands people, forms relationships, solves problems and changes what happens next.

The product combines a freely explorable life-simulation game with cinematic pixel drama. The player can watch a scene, enter it, influence it, leave it or later hear someone else's account. Characters have lives beyond the player's immediate attention. Different players develop different histories, relationships and opportunities.

This is the convergence of the founder's language-learning, autonomous AI-character film, pixel-game and plant-growth ideas. The central experience is not an AI generating unrelated scripts. It is people with persistent identities producing consequential stories through their actions.

The desired reason to return is: **"I want to see what is happening at home"**, not merely "I must finish today's lesson."

A mature experience should feel like this: months after understanding Camille only through gestures, the player can discuss her decision to move to Marseille, remember their first meeting, and express a personal opinion in French. A growing plant and a readable diary make that shared history tangible.

## 2. Player identity and the two-act life

The player is the third roommate, initially a newcomer and student. The game does not assign the player's real identity, gender, feelings or moral positions. It offers a fictional life the player can shape.

### Act I - The Student

A deliberately authored introduction builds attachment, everyday competence and language foundations. The player settles in, attends language school, shops, studies, takes small jobs and participates in a coherent story with meaningful choices.

Core lessons, performances and branching scenes are authored in advance. This act should remain playable without live generative AI. AI may assist production, but every play session is not dependent on a new model response.

The envisioned student arc is approximately two to three months of sustained play, adjustable to actual progress. This is a narrative-length hypothesis, not a promise that any beginner reaches B1 within that time. Graduation closes the authored student story; language ability is assessed separately.

### Act II - The Resident

Graduation opens the larger life, not a reset. The player keeps the same relationships, promises, diary, plant and consequences. Familiar people acquire more open-ended possibilities rather than being replaced by unrelated AI chat characters.

The player chooses work, friendship, creative projects, study, exploration and other interests. New people arrive through plausible connections. NPC goals and relationships generate ongoing events; these events become playable encounters and short dramas.

Open-world access must not depend on perfect grammar or a formal B1 result. Someone still needing A2 support can continue with appropriate assistance. Later learning can pursue B1, B2 and more demanding communication without making unsupported proficiency claims.

Each player has a personal continuity. Multiplayer, shared economies and trading are not required for this vision.

## 3. One world, three forms of interaction

### Explore

The default view is a navigable 2D pixel neighborhood, preferably an oblique overhead view. The player walks into apartments, shops and public spaces, notices changes, handles objects and meets people. Exploration has value without always initiating a quest.

Start the world concept with a dense, interconnected district rather than an empty full-scale Paris: L’Appartement 411, a bakery/cafe, language school, a small shop, a courtyard or garden, and connecting streets. The mature world can expand when places create meaningful relationships and activities.

### Watch and intervene

An important encounter can tighten into cinematic framing: a held shot, a character turning away, the music stopping, a close view of a letter, a pause before a reply. A short drama is an event in the playable world, not a separate video advertisement or a dialogue transcript.

At meaningful beats, the player can speak, act, remain silent, postpone or leave. Intervention changes the scene from its current state; the game must not continue a prewritten ending that contradicts the intervention.

### Talk closely

Sitting in the kitchen or across a cafe table allows intimate conversation, reflection, jokes and more sustained French. Text and voice are alternative participation channels, not identical measures of language skill.

Movement, drama and conversation share the same people, world facts and timeline. Camera changes must preserve orientation and return control clearly. Accessible alternatives include readable dialogue history, replay and reduced-motion presentation.

## 4. Everyday play and time

A session begins with a life situation, not a wall of required assignments. The player can choose a personal intention: buy breakfast, finish a shift, find someone, read a letter or spend time with a friend.

Three session sizes are legitimate:

| Visit | Complete experience |
|---|---|
| Quick visit, roughly 20-60 seconds | Tend the plant, notice a message, save and leave. |
| Small moment, roughly 3-5 minutes | Share a short conversation or resolve one small event. |
| Full session, roughly 15-30 minutes or longer by choice | Explore, watch or change a drama, do a life activity and reflect. |

These are experience targets, not timers or required attendance. Language study and optional replay can extend a visit without making every player finish an hour.

An ordinary day might connect a breakfast order, a disagreement at the bakery, a short work shift and a message from a roommate. Another day can simply contain rain, cooking and reading. Constant emergencies would undermine both credibility and emotional range.

### Language is an action, not an appended quiz

Life activities give comprehension an immediate purpose. At a bakery shift, the player listens to an order, prepares the right items and clarifies an ambiguous request. Misunderstanding creates an opportunity to repair communication, not automatic dismissal. A short message can arrange a meeting; retelling a disagreement can change what a friend believes.

Jobs, errands, shared cooking, study and personal projects provide varied play rather than one endless chat interface. Resources and small home improvements can motivate choices, but financial optimization must not replace the central experience of relationships and communication. Players interested in quiet exploration or observation still have valid routes through the world.

Keep three clocks distinct: real elapsed time, simulated world time and learning progress. Missing real days does not automatically finish lessons or jump the student story forward.

During absence, ordinary lives can advance and yield a concise return summary. Important personal turns should retain a credible opportunity to participate, without freezing every character. Explicitly time-sensitive commitments can have clearly signaled, proportionate consequences. Do not manufacture irreversible losses merely because the player was busy.

## 5. People worth returning to

### Camille Morel

28, museum conservator. Organized, precise and dryly funny. She values rules and shared responsibilities, partly because she cares deeply about keeping relationships secure. A possible job in Marseille creates a real conflict between personal ambition and the home she has built.

Her French is clear and measured. She may naturally reformulate something, but she is a roommate, not a grammar teacher attached to every sentence. Her reserve should soften through shared history, not through accumulating arbitrary friendship points.

### Noé Laurent

27, freelance cook. Inventive, impulsive, warm and disorganized. He wants to be liked and eventually to build something of his own. His mistakes have motives and consequences; he should not repeat the same betrayal just to restart a plot.

He secretly brought home Croissant. His language is playful and conversational, simplified when necessary without erasing his personality. Cooking, jobs and creative plans provide routes into the wider neighborhood.

### Croissant

An ordinary orange cat with recognizable habits. The cat can disrupt routines and bring people together, but does not speak or act as an all-knowing narrator.

### Madame Martin

The landlady, concerned about the apartment and how residents meet shared responsibilities. She has motives and room for negotiation rather than existing only as an antagonist. Conflict over the unexpected cat is fictional house drama, not a statement about real French tenancy law.

### The wider neighborhood

Additional characters should arrive through work, neighbors, performances, study and existing relationships. Inès, a bakery owner, and Jules, a musician, are example expansion seeds rather than mandatory launch characters.

If an owner needs reliable staff and a musician needs a venue, their goals can intersect even without the player. Helping one person, helping both, or buying breakfast and leaving should produce different later possibilities.

Every important character has a stable identity, desires, constraints, relationships, current plans and remembered experiences. Not every passerby needs the same simulation depth. New characters should enrich the existing social world rather than make it endlessly forgetful.

## 6. Autonomous life, memory and causality

The experience requires independent-seeming people, not one continuously running model process per person. Simulation can advance around meaningful events, visits and time changes.

Separate these kinds of state:

| State | What it means |
|---|---|
| World facts | What actually happened; object locations, jobs, commitments and event outcomes. |
| Character knowledge | What a person observed, was told, believes or misunderstands. |
| Relationship history | Shared experiences, trust, unresolved tensions and promises between specific people. |
| Current intentions | What each person is trying to do, why, and what can stop them. |
| Learner evidence | What the player has demonstrated in French, with what assistance. |
| Private reflection | The player's personal writing; not automatically visible to characters. |

A world fact is not automatically common knowledge. Camille cannot know a secret merely because the system knows it. A character can misinterpret an event without the game corrupting the event itself.

An event follows a causal chain: an existing situation creates incompatible goals; characters take plausible actions; the player may intervene; the outcome updates facts and memories; later events inherit that outcome. Repeated scenes cannot silently undo a promise, resurrect a resolved conflict or give an object back to someone who no longer has it.

A story director selects, paces and frames worthwhile events. It cannot freely rewrite reality to create excitement. Character dialogue expresses the current situation; it does not independently commit impossible world changes.

Different lives emerge from accumulated decisions and interactions, not merely from randomizing names or choosing among three final cutscenes. Characters can reconcile, collaborate, leave, arrive or change ambitions when their history supports it.

## 7. Pixel cinema and a coherent visual identity

The founder's principal reference is the emotional expressiveness of *Rhythm Doctor*: pixel performance, timing, sound, framing and surprising but purposeful presentation. The game needs an original visual identity, not copied characters, music, interfaces or scenes.

The world should feel inhabited: notes on the fridge, a cup left on a table, shifting weather, a familiar window and a plant that has changed since arrival. Recurring objects can carry story meaning.

AI-generated drama should primarily become **performable scene instructions**, not a newly generated video for every interaction. A scene selects an existing place and cast, lines, poses, movement, timing, sound and intervention beats. A renderer performs these with reusable assets.

Shared proportions, palette, pixel grid and animation vocabulary preserve consistency. Close dialogue views may use dedicated portraits rather than pretending that zooming an overhead sprite creates a new perspective. Add art only when it serves expression or interaction.

Distinct gestures can support character identity, but must not mechanically reveal truth or replace language. A familiar nervous movement can be a clue, not an infallible lie detector.

French accents, subtitles and Chinese support remain readable. Pixel art is for the world; tiny unreadable pixel fonts are not a requirement. Sound aids comprehension and emotion, with text alternatives and controllable playback.

## 8. The language-learning system

### Principle: progressively comprehensible input

French is encountered because the player wants to understand something meaningful. Begin with supported understanding and gradually test whether the language itself carries meaning.

The intended progression is:

**Meaningful situation -> sound/text linked to meaning -> repeated encounters with variation -> reduced dependence on clues -> understanding in a new context -> increasingly voluntary expression.**

This is the product's teaching design, not a claim that every learner follows one universal sequence. Input is central; interaction, retrieval, focused explanations, writing and feedback support deeper development.

A gesture can make a request understandable. That is useful teaching. It is not yet proof that the player understood the French. Later, several actions should be plausible and the linguistic distinction should determine which one fits.

### Adaptive support, not an immersion switch

Support is tracked by expression and skill, not turned off for everyone on a particular day. One learner can understand greetings by ear while still needing translation for location phrases.

Available support includes meaningful animation, clear audio, replay, French subtitles, a word or phrase gloss, a Chinese explanation, an example and a short structural note. Help remains available throughout the game.

After repeated evidence in distinct contexts, try removing one default support at a time. Restore support when understanding breaks down. Do not simultaneously accelerate speech, hide subtitles and introduce unfamiliar words, then interpret failure as regression.

Complex personalities need not require inaccessible language. Preserve intentions and personality while adjusting wording, turn length and support. Learning should open greater nuance, not determine whether the player deserves a full story.

### A continuous curriculum beneath an open life

The authored student period develops practical communication from arrival through belonging and independence. Typical functions include introducing oneself, finding objects, ordering, explaining plans, recounting events, expressing reasons and negotiating misunderstandings.

The intended progression is a design map, not automatic level certification:

| Life phase | Communication depth |
|---|---|
| Arrival, broadly Pre-A1/A1 | Recognize people and objects; understand brief requests; introduce oneself and meet basic needs. |
| Belonging, broadly A1/A2 | Manage routines, preferences, plans, simple accounts of events and everyday problems. |
| Independence, working toward B1 | Give a connected account, explain reasons, compare options and help resolve a misunderstanding. |
| Resident life, toward B2 and beyond | Understand less explicit intentions, discuss competing views, sustain complex relationships and handle more demanding work or study. |

The resident world continues these abilities through work, relationships, reading, problems and projects. A learning director notices suitable opportunities and neglected skills. It adjusts presentation and invites optional practice; it does not force NPCs to create implausible situations just to use a target word.

Environmental print, messages, menus, letters and diaries provide reading. Spoken scenes, orders and messages provide listening. Conversation, retelling and clarification provide interaction and production. Personal notes and diaries develop writing. Focused sound or grammar practice remains available when incidental exposure is insufficient.

Input, output, fluency and explicit attention should not disappear simply because one play style dominates. Someone who only reads needs invitations to listen; someone who only watches needs opportunities to recall and communicate, without being forced into speech on every visit.

### Contextual repetition and feedback

Revisit useful language across people, locations and time. A request for help may first concern a box, later a work shift, then a personal problem. Increasing familiarity should permit less support and richer expression.

Schedule delayed encounters, but do not make dialogue unnatural to empty a review queue. Missed practice can be deferred or offered through the diary. There is no punitive backlog.

Understandable mistakes can receive a natural reformulation while the scene continues. When meaning breaks, offer a small hint before a complete answer. Distinguish a player's intended action from the exact sentence form; story morality is not a language score.

Speech recognition may support communication but is not by itself proof of pronunciation quality. More advanced sound and fluency work needs appropriate evidence and honest limits.

### Learning evidence and outcomes

Track supported comprehension, language-dependent discrimination, transfer, delayed retention and independent production separately. Keep listening, reading, typed writing and spoken interaction distinct.

One correct click can be a guess. Repeating a familiar recording is not the same as understanding a new speaker. A sentence supplied by the system is not the player's writing. With no audio evidence, listening remains unverified.

Occasional checkpoints use familiar language in previously unseen combinations or settings. Record the support and conditions, not just a score. An initial baseline matters, especially when the founder already knows some French.

Progress should be expressible as concrete abilities: understanding a short request without translation, following a new location description, retelling an event or explaining a preference. The game does not award a formal CEFR level merely for completing a chapter. Claims of broader learning effectiveness require evaluation beyond one enthusiastic founder.

## 9. A diary made from the player's life

The diary is both an emotional archive and a personal learning library.

**Chronicle:** records significant events and choices that actually occurred, with a scene image or other small memento where useful. It respects what was observed, reported or uncertain. It does not give away unseen secrets merely because a narrator could access them.

**Private journal:** preserves the player's own feelings and wording. Corrections or rewritten versions sit alongside the original, never silently replace it or invent the player's emotions. Characters learn private thoughts only when the player chooses to share them in-world.

The same event can be retold at different language levels without changing its facts. Later the player can read an old entry, listen to a retelling, explain it to a friend or write a new perspective. A received letter can reveal another character's viewpoint through a plausible story event, not unrestricted access to everyone's inner thoughts.

Short personal episodes may eventually be replayed as pixel scenes. These are representations of the player's history, not manufactured memories. Export and privacy controls preserve ownership of that history.

## 10. A plant that belongs to this life

Each player owns a plant from early in the story. It is a low-effort reason to return, a visible passage of time and an object of attachment.

Keep **care** and **growth** separate. Care reflects tending and absence. Growth and decorations reflect lived experiences and milestones. Completing a meaningful chapter, receiving a gift or graduating can leave a visual mark without treating one moral choice as the correct answer.

An unattended plant can become thirsty, droop and eventually look wilted or dormant. This consequence should be visible and meaningful, but recoverable. Coming back after a difficult month must not erase months of history. Exact timings are tuning decisions, not fixed promises or claims about real botany.

A roommate may occasionally help when the relationship and circumstances support it. This creates reciprocity without guaranteeing automatic care. Tending the plant never requires passing a language test or purchasing a rescue.

A quick watering visit can reveal a message or a genuine invitation from a roommate. The player may stay because the situation is interesting, but can also finish watering and leave. Do not turn every visit into a compulsory crisis.

Over time, varieties, pots, gifts and small differences make plant corners personal. The original plant can accompany the player into a new home after graduation. It becomes evidence of a life, not merely a streak counter.

## 11. How the complete experience runs

The world first establishes what happened and who knows it. The director chooses a worthwhile situation. The learning layer chooses accessible phrasing and support without changing those facts. The performance layer stages the encounter. The player observes or acts; valid consequences update the world. The diary records the experience, the learner model records the evidence, and the next visit inherits both.

A scene therefore feeds three continuities at once: a life that changes, relationships that remember, and French that becomes more understandable. Plant care can open this loop in seconds; a longer session can follow it through a complete drama. None of the systems should need to invent a separate reason for the player to care.

## 12. Lasting product principles

- Fun and attachment are essential; time spent alone is not evidence of learning or well-being.
- The player can observe, participate or leave. Language errors do not remove agency.
- New stories inherit old facts, relationships and learning history.
- Private reflection is not automatically character knowledge or development telemetry.
- The game should survive pauses, resume faithfully and keep old progress usable through updates.
- Authored scenes and generative scenes must feel like parts of the same world.
- The mature product is judged by believable continuity, enjoyable participation and demonstrable French development, not the number of autonomous agents.

**The ultimate experience: a world the player first struggles to understand becomes a place in which they can genuinely express a life.**

# First Week - Progressive Comprehensible Input

Content baseline for Stage 01. French examples are authored draft material to review before release, not a claim of expert validation. Seven "days" are chapters; absence never skips instruction.

## Learning contract

Build a small foundation from no assumed French: connect sound with meaning, recognize a few useful functions, transfer some familiar language and begin optional short expression. Do not claim A1 after seven episodes.

Before the first episode, offer a brief, skippable baseline using a few comparable comprehension items. The founder may already know French. Record that starting point and permit lighter support; one founder is not a representative zero-beginner sample. No penalty or label is attached to a baseline miss.

Use three content labels:

- **Understand:** a target for meaningful comprehension and later checks.
- **Try:** short optional player expression, with or without support.
- **Atmosphere:** language that enriches a scene but is not silently added to assessed requirements.

Teach through context first. Later, check with multiple plausible interpretations and no clue that gives away the answer. Introduce the required nouns and task mechanics before testing a structure. Unknown vocabulary, a new control or a new camera angle must not be mistaken for lack of language knowledge.

Keep most turns short. Requesting repetition is a legitimate communicative action. No correct phrase, translation or gesture should flash before an item labeled independent.

## Assistance and recurrence

| Support | Default presentation |
|---|---|
| 3 - Strong | Clear audio, French subtitles, meaningful visual demonstration and brief Chinese explanation when needed. |
| 2 - Moderate | Audio and French subtitles; natural scene support; Chinese on request. |
| 1 - Light | Audio first; subtitles and meaning help on request; no answer-highlighting. |
| 0 - Independent trial | Familiar language without answer-revealing support; replay and help always available. |

Maintain support per target and modality. Two independent successes in distinct contexts can trigger a one-level trial reduction, not a mastery declaration. Seek delayed evidence before treating learning as stable. Restore support after difficulty or a request; never interpret help as misconduct.

Use new context and one changed factor at a time. Actual played French audio is required for listening evidence; typed output remains writing. A replay without revealed meaning can remain a no-translation attempt but is recorded separately from first-listen success.

A simple starting review schedule can suggest encounters around 1, 3, 7 and 14 days after introduction, adjusted by evidence. It is a tuning heuristic. In the first week use the chapter recurrences below; when absences create a backlog, prioritize a couple of useful items rather than forcing all of them into a scene.

## Day 1 - Bienvenue chez nous

**Story:** arrival, two roommates, a plant and an unexpected cat.

**Understand:** `Salut !`, `Bonjour !`, `Je m'appelle...`, `Tu t'appelles comment ?`  
**Try:** `Je m'appelle [nickname].` A nickname-only response can communicate but is not evidence of producing the full structure.  
**Atmosphere:** `Moi, c'est Noé.`, `C'est ta plante.`, `Ta plante a soif.`, `C'est Croissant.`, `Un secret ?`

### Playable sequence

1. **Doorway.** Camille points to herself: `Salut ! Je m'appelle Camille.` Her name and action support meaning. Noé appears: `Salut ! Je m'appelle Noé.` Use the same target frame before introducing the atmospheric variant. The player sees who is speaking and can replay.
2. **Your introduction.** Camille asks `Tu t'appelles comment ?` A brief Chinese explanation is available. Accept a nickname, optional sentence attempt or a skipped response. Do not infer gender or insist on real personal data.
3. **Plant corner.** Camille shows the plant and a care action. The player can water and pause. The environment teaches meaning; this action is not a test of `plante` or `soif`.
4. **Cat.** Camille leaves before the cat appears. Noé says `C'est Croissant.` and asks for secrecy. Use supported choices: agree to keep the secret or decline. Save the actual choice; Camille does not acquire knowledge from an unseen scene.
5. **Small recognition check.** Present familiar introduction and name-question audio in a neutral arrangement. Let the player distinguish "the person tells me their name" from "the person asks for mine," then choose or give an appropriate response. Remove pointing, name highlighting and other answer cues. Vary order and known speakers; do not claim broad transfer from these few examples.
6. **Diary and exit.** The chronicle records only encountered events. The player may write `Je m'appelle...`, a personal line or nothing. Save and resume from the actual checkpoint.

**Later recurrence:** greetings and self-introduction when meeting the neighbor and landlady.  
**Minimum evidence:** source of understanding, support used and an optional original response. A name-matching click with visible labels is context-supported, not independent listening.

## Day 2 - Le petit-déjeuner

**Story:** Noé prepares breakfast while Camille wants the right drink.

**Understand:** `du thé`, `du café`, `Tu veux du thé ou du café ?`, `Merci !`  
**Try:** `Je voudrais du thé.` / `Je voudrais du café.`

Make the two drinks perceptually clear during teaching. Pair a short request, audio, action and result. Accept the player's preference, not a predetermined "correct" preference. Introduce a simple change of request with support before asking the player to interpret it independently.

**Probe:** both drinks remain equally selectable and positions change. The final spoken request determines which to serve; no color, gaze or glowing cup identifies the requested one. Use only established drink words. A supported correction lets the breakfast continue.

**Diary:** let the player choose or write their drink preference; an offered complete sentence is supported writing, not independent composition.  
**Recurrence:** greetings return; drink requests can reappear in a later scene or optional replay. Do not confuse recognizing the word for coffee with knowing that the same written word can name a cafe venue.

## Day 3 - Le colis mystérieux

**Story:** a parcel for Camille has been left somewhere in the apartment. The eventual Marseille clue remains a story detail, not required beginner vocabulary.

**Understand:** `Où est le colis ?`, `Le colis est sur la table.`, `Le colis est sous la table.`  
**Try:** `Sur la table.` / `Sous la table.`

First establish `le colis` and `la table` through objects and audio. Demonstrate the same parcel being above and below the same table; change one relation, not the entire scene and vocabulary at once.

**Probe:** a voice note gives the parcel's location in a room the player has not yet searched. Both above and below the table are plausible choices. The parcel is revealed only after the choice, so its visible position cannot supply the answer. A later variant changes the layout while keeping known nouns.

**Diary:** a factual location sentence, copied, selected or independently written with its support status preserved.  
**Recurrence:** location expressions return with the keys and cat. Introduce any new noun before assessing the old relation.

## Day 4 - La voisine en colère

**Story:** noise brings a neighbor to the door. The player can understand a complaint without being required to choose one moral response.

**Understand:** `Il y a du bruit.`, `Pardon.`, `Tu peux répéter ?`  
**Try:** a brief apology or a request to repeat. Offer `Vous pouvez répéter, s'il vous plaît ?` as a supported polite alternative, not a surprise new test.

Use the sound and the situation to establish `bruit`, then let the neighbor repeat more clearly. An angry face alone does not establish the meaning of the sentence. Pausing or asking for help remains available.

**Probe:** in a neutral message context, distinguish the familiar complaint from a previously taught question such as `Où est le colis ?` Ask which issue the message concerns; do not let tone or facial animation be the only cue. Keep any action choice separate from this comprehension result.

**Diary:** a short factual note and optional personal reaction. Do not make the system choose the player's feelings.  
**Recurrence:** greetings from a different person, requests for clarification throughout later episodes.

## Day 5 - Les clés perdues

**Story:** Noé cannot find his keys.

**Understand:** `Je cherche mes clés.`, `Je cherche...`, `Je ne sais pas.`  
**Try:** asking where a familiar object is, stating a location, or saying that the player does not know.  
**Atmosphere:** `J'ai perdu mes clés.` Only supported comprehension is expected; do not require independent past-tense production.

Establish what the keys are before the search. Reuse `sur` and `sous` in a different room. The story offers an information gap: Noé lacks a location the player may discover, not a teacher demanding the password to continue.

**Probe:** understand a location message about a now-familiar object, with no pointing or highlighted answer. Separately introduce a new request involving the already familiar parcel to see whether `Je cherche...` transfers beyond keys.

**Diary:** factual account of the search. More complex auto-written narration remains reading input, not learner achievement.  
**Recurrence:** a delayed check of Day 3's relations and preparation for Day 6's search.

## Day 6 - Où est Croissant ?

**Story:** Croissant has wandered outside; the player asks for help beyond the apartment.

**Understand:** `Je cherche mon chat.`, `À gauche.`, `À droite.` plus familiar location questions.  
**Try:** explain the search in a short phrase or request repetition.

Establish `mon chat` and the new directions through supported demonstrations. Moving into the street is a new context, not permission to introduce a large unexplained vocabulary list. A new speaker can be clear and slow rather than suddenly much harder.

**Probe:** a passerby gives a short direction at a genuine choice point. Both routes are plausible; there is no pointing hand or route highlight. Keep navigation mechanics familiar. Test direction and search-phrase transfer separately so a failure has an interpretable cause.

**Diary:** record where the cat was actually found. Offer a small retelling, not mandatory advanced narration.  
**Recurrence:** `Je cherche...`, `Où est... ?`, clarification and previously established spatial language.

## Day 7 - L'inspection

**Story:** Madame Martin visits. The cat and previous choices matter. The player may negotiate, remain uncertain, tell the truth or avoid the topic; these decisions are not language correctness labels.

**Understand:** recombinations of familiar language, including `Il y a un chat ici ?` and `Où est le chat ?`, with `ici` introduced in context before any check.  
**Try:** a short relevant response, greeting, request or clarification.  
**Atmosphere:** `Je voudrais vous parler.` is optional supported input, not a new compulsory output target.

Use a new character/voice without simultaneously adding fast speech and unfamiliar grammar. Carry forward character knowledge honestly. A player who declined secrecy on Day 1 must not be credited with a promise they never made.

Separate the story scene from the small formative check below. Help remains available and the player proceeds regardless of score. An inconclusive result means continued support, not exclusion from the next story.

**Diary:** a short first-week account assembled from actual events, plus optional player writing. Offer replay or reading at a suitable level. Mark unfamiliar connective language as supported input.

## First-week learning check

Use previously unheard combinations or equivalent alternate forms, not the same item immediately after showing its answer. Keep prerequisites familiar and record content/test versions. A new word cannot quietly make a supposed transfer check harder.

| Evidence | Design |
|---|---|
| Listening discrimination | Eight short trials across practiced functions, with plausible choices and no visible answer. Record first-listen, replay, text help and translation separately. |
| Transfer | Four of those trials change a known speaker, arrangement or setting without introducing an unlearned language demand. |
| Reading | One new short note made from familiar language; locate the relevant information. |
| Production | Invite two short original communicative responses and one short written message. Distinguish independent, partial-template and full-template output; speech is optional and separately recorded. |
| Retention | Revisit a small parallel set after roughly 48 hours or at the next suitable later visit; record elapsed time and interim practice. |

Initial pilot targets can be six of eight comprehension trials and three of four transfer trials without translation or answer-revealing cues. Report first-listen and replay-assisted results separately. These small-sample targets are tuning aids, not validated cut scores or proof of mastery; include unsuccessful and inconclusive evidence.

A learner who requests help receives it and completes the story. Reassess later with an alternate item rather than relabeling the now-explained answer independent.

If reliable audio was unavailable, report a reading-only result and unverified listening. If no speech was captured, report no spoken-production conclusion. A complete sentence supplied by the game never counts as independent writing.

## Release check for each episode

The Experience owner verifies the target phrases, intended meanings, prerequisite vocabulary, actual voice/text alignment, supportive teaching context and a non-leaking probe. Freeze the released content version; keep substantive changes identifiable.

Use exposure as evidence of an encounter, not proof of acquisition. The question after the week is: **What can this learner now understand or express under conditions they could not handle at baseline, and what evidence supports that?**
